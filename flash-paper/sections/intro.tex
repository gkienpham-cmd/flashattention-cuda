\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Large language model (LLM) serving latency is dominated by attention
\emph{decode}: after the prompt is processed, tokens are generated one at a time,
and each new token attends over the entire growing key--value (KV) cache. Because
a single query row ($N_q{=}1$) reads a large KV cache to produce one output, decode
is the archetypal memory-bound, low-arithmetic-intensity workload, and it is where
production systems spend most of their wall-clock time. Highly tuned kernels---
FlashInfer~\cite{flashinfer}, FlashMLA~\cite{flashmla}, and the FlashAttention
lineage~\cite{fa1,fa2,fa3,fa4}---are deployed on NVIDIA's Blackwell GPUs, but the
open literature contains no \emph{prediction-vs-measured roofline}
characterization of attention decode on the latest datacenter part
(B300 / GB300, \texttt{sm\_103}). Production write-ups report headline throughput;
they do not publish, per kernel, what the roofline predicted, what was measured,
and \emph{why} the two differ.

\paragraph{The empty cell}
Figure~\ref{fig:landscape} maps the landscape. FlashAttention-4~\cite{fa4} targets
and benchmarks B200 (\texttt{sm\_100}) for BF16 prefill/training; its kernel is
deployed but never \emph{characterized} on \texttt{sm\_103}. The closest prior
open roofline of MLA-style decode is Grouped-Latent Attention (GLA) on
H100~\cite{gla}; our delta is exactly \texttt{sm\_103} plus KV-quantization.
SnapMLA~\cite{snapmla} studies FP8 MLA decode on Hopper without a Blackwell
roofline; SageAttention3~\cite{sage3} and Attn-QAT~\cite{attnqat} push FP4
attention but for \emph{prefill}. Microbenchmark-driven analytical
modeling~\cite{roofmethod} establishes \emph{why} naive roofline fails on modern
GPUs (it owns the method---we cite and apply it). The cell for an open,
prediction-vs-measured decode roofline on \texttt{sm\_103}, across attention
types and precisions, is empty. This paper fills it.

\paragraph{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The first open, prediction-vs-measured roofline characterization of
  attention \emph{decode} on B300 / \texttt{sm\_103}, spanning three attention
  types (MHA $\to$ GQA $\to$ MLA) and three precisions (FP16 $\to$ FP8 $\to$
  NVFP4), built as twelve single-variable kernel steps.
  \item A \emph{two-layer} prediction model---pure roofline plus a
  per-CTA-corrected layer---with predictions \emph{pre-registered before} each
  kernel is coded or measured. The gap between the layers is itself the finding:
  across the seven decode steps the per-CTA-corrected layer matched the measured
  limiter every time, while pure roofline predicted the wrong regime at every
  single-stream step (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
  \item The central finding: decode's per-CTA ``wall'' is
  \emph{work-starvation}, not a fixed architectural limit. The right engine
  converts work into throughput---a warp-per-head CUDA-core MLA kernel is flat at
  $0.75\tflops$, whereas the production \texttt{tcgen05} kernel spans
  $2.4 \to 1785\tflops$ scaling with batch $\times$ context---but the roofs are
  reachable only at serving scale, not in common single-stream decode.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Honest scope}
We \emph{complement}, not beat, production kernels: FlashInfer, FlashMLA, and the
CUTLASS reference kernel already run B300 decode and are faster by construction.
The prediction-vs-measured \emph{methodology} is also not novel---prior work owns
it~\cite{roofmethod}. Our contribution is the open \emph{application} of that
methodology to this workload $\times$ architecture $\times$ precision space, the
per-CTA-corrected model, and the boundary results (including a negative one,
Section~\ref{sec:stagea}). Figure~\ref{fig:positioning} states precisely what we
claim and what we do not. Prediction \emph{misses} are reported as first-class
results throughout.
